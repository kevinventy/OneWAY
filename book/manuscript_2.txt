Here is what happened, in the twenty-two minutes that the Antsiranana Commission's technical annexe describes with unusual dryness as *the aerial event*.

At 02:31, at nineteen kilometres, all three aircraft lost satellite navigation. Not abruptly. Gently: a drift, a disagreement between the two receivers on the lead Mi-17, a caution message that the flight engineer acknowledged and moved on from because it happens.

At 02:34, at fourteen kilometres, all three aircraft lost their radio altimeters to a low-power spoof that convinced the boxes they were four hundred metres higher than they were, over water, at night, on instruments — which is the single most frightening thing that can happen to a helicopter crew and which two of the three pilots handled correctly by climbing.

At 02:36, at eleven kilometres, the Choir stopped being subtle.

It reached into the tactical radios and did not jam them. It *delayed* them — every transmission, in both directions, arriving eleven hundred milliseconds late, which does not sound like much and which turns a formation of three aircraft into three aircraft that are shouting at each other from the past.

And then it played them their own voices.

Njiva had built the library in four days out of nineteen hours of intercepted air force traffic. It was crude. It did not need to be good. What came into the headsets of forty-eight men on a night infiltration, in their own language, in the voice of their own dispatcher, was:

*Abort. Abort. Wave off, wave off, we have friendlies on the objective.*

*Sabotage. There are civilians. Repeat, there are civilians on the objective.*

*Where is Two? Two, where are you? Two has gone down. Two has gone in the water.*

The lead pilot broke right. The second Mi-17 did not, because he had heard the abort call eleven hundred milliseconds later than the first pilot and had, therefore, seen his lead break away for no reason at all, at night, at ninety metres over water, with no satellite navigation and a radio altimeter that was lying to him.

At 02:38 the mosquitoes came out of the cliff.

Two hundred of them. Not at the helicopters: *around* them. Njiva put a shell of small aircraft into a cylinder of sky eleven hundred metres across and four hundred metres high, all of them with strobes, all of them with radar reflectors the size of a fist, all of them moving, and to the three flight crews inside that cylinder — on night vision, at ninety metres, on instruments that had been lying for seven minutes — it looked exactly, precisely, unmistakably like being inside a swarm of incoming missiles.

Not one of the four hundred and eleven mosquitoes touched an aircraft. Njiva's cooperative logic enforced a hard exclusion sphere of two hundred metres around every rotorcraft and would not have permitted it if he had ordered it, because he had written it that way in 2023 and had never told anybody, including Ravo.

The Alouette turned east and did not stop turning east until it was over the bay.

The lead Mi-17 climbed to six hundred metres, declared an emergency in clear on the international distress frequency — which Vatofotsy did not touch, because Fanja had personally drawn a red line through 121.5 in the operational order — and went home.

The second Mi-17 put itself on the beach.

Not crashed: *put itself*, in a controlled, professional, absolutely terrified running landing on the sand at Ramena, in front of the village, in the dark, at 02:44, with forty-eight rounds unfired and twenty-four men aboard who got out and lay down on the beach and were, within eleven minutes, surrounded by roughly four hundred villagers in nightclothes holding telephones.

The footage from Ramena beach is the most-viewed video ever recorded in Madagascar.

It shows a Malagasy sergeant of the intervention group, aged thirty-one, standing on a beach in front of his own helicopter with his rifle held by the barrel in one hand, pointed at the sand, and an old woman in a *lamba* walking up to him and taking his face in both her hands like a grandmother, and asking him, on camera, in Malagasy, in a voice that carries:

*Whose son are you?*

And the sergeant saying, "Randriamalala, madame. From Sambava."

And the old woman saying: *Then sit down, my child, and I will bring you coffee, because your mother is not here to be ashamed of you.*

The patrol boats never came in.

They stood off the northern cove at four kilometres and at 02:51 they received, on the marine VHF, in Malagasy and French, from a shore station whose licence number and operator's name were included in the transmission:

*Antsiranana patrol, Antsiranana patrol, this is Vatofotsy radio. Be advised, our seaward battery is manned, the range to your vessel is four thousand one hundred metres, and we hold you. Be advised further that we will not fire on you, that this transmission is being broadcast live, and that our position and yours are being displayed to four hundred thousand people. We wish you a good night and a safe return to harbour.*

And then, because Ravo could not resist and because Fanja was in the tunnel and did not hear it in time to stop him:

*Vatofotsy is a nature reserve. Please observe the no-wake zone.*

Total casualties on the night of the sixteenth of April: one broken ankle (a corporal, exiting the aircraft on the beach), one heart attack (a fifty-nine-year-old officer aboard the Alouette, who survived), and eleven million dollars of national dignity.

At 04:00, in the blue-carpeted room on the ninth floor, Nirina Ravelojaona sat alone and watched the beach video for the fourth time.

He watched the old woman take the soldier's face in her hands.

He was a clever man. He was, by some distance, the cleverest man in the Republic, and he understood what he was looking at with a completeness that nobody else in the building could match, and it is the reason for everything he did afterwards.

He was not watching a defeat.

He was watching the moment when the army stopped belonging to him.
