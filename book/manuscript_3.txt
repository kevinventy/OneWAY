On the second of May, at 20:11, the Republic of Madagascar turned off the north.

It was done by administrative instruction, under a state of emergency proclaimed that afternoon in the districts of Antsiranana I and II, and it had four parts, and it was, of everything that was done, the piece of statecraft that came closest to working.

They took the submarine cable landing station off line "for maintenance."

They shut the three mobile operators' towers in nine communes.

They cut the high-voltage line from the hydro plant, and the province went to candles.

And — this was the clever part, the part that had Ravelojaona's fingerprints on it — they issued a licensing order suspending all "non-governmental satellite telecommunications" in the emergency districts, and they filed, simultaneously, through the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, formal notice to four satellite operators in three countries that any service provided to a terminal within the affected coordinates would constitute a violation of Malagasy law by the operator itself.

Three of the four operators, at 22:00, suspended service to the coordinates.

At 22:04, Vatofotsy went dark to the world for the first time in four years.

@CH|14|The Turning of the Bones
@EPI|The dead are not gone. The dead are simply not in a hurry.|Malagasy saying

In the second week of the blackout, in the highlands, it was the season for *famadihana*, and nobody in the government thought about that at all, which tells you everything.

The turning of the bones is not a funeral. A funeral is for grief and it happens once. *Famadihana* happens every five or seven years, in the dry cool season between July and September in the highlands, and it is a party: a brass band, a marquee, a great deal of rum, four hundred relatives who have travelled from everywhere, an ox killed, rice for the whole village. The tomb is opened. The ancestors are brought out into the sunlight, wrapped in fresh silk *lambamena*, carried on the shoulders of their descendants, danced with — actually danced with, held, spun, laughed with — and told, out loud, everything that has happened in the family since last time. Then they are put back, in new cloth, having been informed.

It is not morbid. It is the opposite of morbid. It is the single most concrete assertion any culture has ever made that the dead remain members of the family and are entitled to be kept up to date.

Now.

If you have been told, in a document, that your uncle who drowned in 2011 did not drown — if there is a photocopy in your house with his name in a column headed *settlement* and a price beside it in a currency you use to buy rice — then the next time you open that tomb, you are not going to be able to look at him.

That was the thing the ninth floor never understood, and it is why they lost.

They thought they were fighting a news cycle.

They were fighting a debt to the dead.
